Implementing a multi-agent workflow was an interesting challenge. Ii was forced into mre   of a system designer role, and had to contend with the fact that I was "programming" mainly in English, which was weird (like what that one open ai guy said). The biggest lesson was how much clarity comes from decomposing the task into two distinct roles with different responsibilities and incentives. The Planner focuses on coverage, pacing, and budget structure, while the Reviewer acts as a skeptical auditor that hunts for conflicts, bad assumptions, and feasibility gaps. By writing the interaction expectations of what was to be assumed for both of them, I was able to be proactive and catch any issues that could arise. It was a noble way to attempt to increase trust between the models output and myself, though it is guilty of the "who watches the watchmen" issue (I.e what validates the validator?)

The main challenges were (1) preventing vague feedback from the Reviewer, (2) keeping the itinerary grounded in time/budget reality, and (3) making tool usage observable without leaking secrets. I addressed (1) by enforcing a “Delta List” of concrete edits instead of general advice. For (2), I had the Planner default to conservative cost ranges, cluster nearby activities, and add buffer time around transfers—so the Reviewer mostly fine-tunes instead of rewriting. For (3), I added a simple tool-event logger in the sidebar with redaction of sensitive values and short previews, plus a testing-keys override that mirrors into the environment for local runs.

Design-wise, I gave the Planner the persona of a calm, practical concierge whose job is to produce a pleasant, evenly paced plan that respects constraints and marks uncertainties. The Reviewer is a meticulous QA analyst who validates assumptions, cites evidence by source title, and only proposes minimal, targeted changes to fix concrete issues. I chose Markdown over JSON for readability and made the final output the Corrected Itinerary, keeping the raw plan in an expander for transparency. Finally, I biased the system toward official sources, small safe edits, and clear failure modes, which together made the pipeline feel dependable and easy to reason about.
